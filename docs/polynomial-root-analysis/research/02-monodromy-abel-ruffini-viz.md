# Research note 02 — Visualising monodromy, Arnold's Abel–Ruffini proof, and the Galois correspondence

_Compiled 2026-09-22 for the planned interactive app (TypeScript + WebGL; roots drawn in ℂ, draggable
coefficients, loops in coefficient space animating root permutations)._

Method: web search + fetching every primary source that could be reached; PDFs were text-extracted
(Ramond, Goldmakher, Żołądek, Kalicki–Morales–Ostrander, Vrij, Skopenkov, Khovanskii's appendix, the UIC
poster, the MIT PRIMES slides); the JavaScript of the four live demos (Stein, Akalin, Ostrander, relint.de)
was downloaded and read to see _how_ they animate. Where a claim is from a secondary source (search
snippet, review) rather than the page itself, it is marked "(secondary)". YouTube pages could not be
fetched from this container (consent wall), so video contents are taken from the papers that cite them and
from their oEmbed metadata.

## 0. One-paragraph verdict

The topological proof has a small, stable literature (Arnold 1963 → Alekseev 2004 → Żołądek 2000 →
Katz video c. 2020 → Goldmakher notes → Ramond 2020 → a handful of blogs/theses) and exactly **four
live browser demos** (Stein 2019, Akalin 2016, Kalicki–Morales–Ostrander 2019, relint.de 2026). All four
draw roots and coefficients as dots in two complex planes and animate a _swap of roots_ (roots are
moved; coefficients follow by Vieta). **None of them lets the user draw a loop in coefficient space and
tracks the roots along it with a continuation method**; none draws the discriminant locus; none shows a
nested commutator as a structured object (a tree of loops); none shows the Galois correspondence or an
intermediate field numerically; none uses the GPU. Akalin (2016) is the most complete interactive
treatment (six JSXGraph examples through the quintic, a "rotation counter" that tracks radical branches,
a button that finds the operation ruling out a candidate formula); Katz's video is the canonical
animated exposition; Ramond's paper is the cleanest written step list with explicit permutations. The app
can adopt their mechanisms (§9) and add the things none of them do (§8).

### 1.3 The proof structure the app should follow (synthesised, with the explicit data)

Notation: monic p(z) = zⁿ + cₙ₋₁zⁿ⁻¹ + … + c₀ with roots s₁…sₙ; **coefficient space** 𝔽ₙ = {c ∈ ℂⁿ : disc ≠ 0} (Goldmakher's Fₙ; Arnold's space of polynomials without multiple roots); **root configuration space** UConfₙ(ℂ). Vieta: 𝔽ₙ ≅ UConfₙ(ℂ), and π₁(𝔽ₙ) = Bₙ (Arnold 1970; Żołądek Rem. 2).

**Step 0 (roots move continuously).** Roots depend continuously on coefficients; along a path in 𝔽ₙ they never collide, so a loop induces a permutation (the monodromy). Draw: two panes, coefficients left, roots right; drag c₀ around a small loop → roots return; around a big loop that encloses a root of the discriminant (as a polynomial in c₀) → two roots swap. (Goldmakher §2 with Stein's tool: degree 2, a₀=−1, a₁=½, roots ≈ −1.28, 0.78; degree 3: a₀ = −½+½i, a₁ = 0, a₂ = −½−½i, roots −½−½i, i, 1; a loop of a₀ gives () or (1 2 3).)

**Step 1 (every permutation is a monodromy).** For any σ ∈ Sₙ move the roots along non-crossing paths realising σ (a transposition = a lens of two arcs; a 3-cycle = three arcs; others = products); by Vieta each coefficient traces a closed loop; concatenation of loops ↔ product of permutations (note the order reversal, Vrij Prop. 4.2). So the monodromy of the _general_ equation is all of Sₙ. (Goldmakher Prop. 5; Ramond Fig. 4; Żołądek Lemma 1–2 give the branch-point version: monodromy is generated by the transpositions around the n−1 discriminant points seen when only c₀ varies, and is transitive.)

**Step 2 (F-functions follow loops).** Anything built from the cᵢ with +,−,×,÷ (and, per Goldmakher/Khovanskii, any single-valued continuous function: exp, sin, cos, …) returns to its value along any loop. Hence no such formula can output a root: apply (1 2) and the formula's value comes back while s₁,s₂ have swapped. (Ramond "first impossibility result", Fig. 5; Goldmakher Prop. 1/Cor. 2.)

**Step 3 (radicals do not follow loops, but follow commutators).** If w = ᵏ√F and F winds m times around 0, the k values of w (equally spaced on the circle |w| = |F|^{1/k}) each slide m places (Ramond Fig. 2). Along a **commutator loop** [γ₁,γ₂] = γ₁γ₂γ₁⁻¹γ₂⁻¹ the winding of F is m₁+m₂−m₁−m₂ = 0, so every branch of ᵏ√F closes (Vrij Lemma 5.8; Goldmakher Ex. 1; Kalicki Claim 3.3 "the cyclic permutations of the k radicals commute"). Draw: the path of one branch of √F consists of four unclosed pieces that nevertheless close (Ramond Fig. 6, right). **Key lemma:** _a G-function (one level of radicals over F-functions) returns to itself along any commutator of loops in 𝔽ₙ._

**Step 4 (cubic).** [(12),(23)] = (123) (Ramond eq. 15; relint writes (123) = (12)(23)(12)⁻¹(23)⁻¹). Realise (12) and (23) by loops γ₁, γ₂ (Step 1); along [γ₁,γ₂] the roots undergo (123) while every G-function closes ⇒ **the cubic formula needs two levels of radicals** (Cardano: ³√(−Q ± √(Q²+P³))). _Solvability seen positively:_ S₃' = A₃ = ⟨(123)⟩ is abelian, so [A₃,A₃] = 1 — a commutator of commutators is trivial, which is why two levels suffice.

**Step 5 (quartic).** [(123),(234)] = (14)(23), i.e. [(ijk),(jkℓ)] = (iℓ)(jk) (Ramond eq. 21; relint: (ij)(kℓ) = (ikℓ)(kℓj)(ikℓ)⁻¹(kℓj)⁻¹). Writing each 3-cycle itself as a commutator of transpositions gives the **doubly nested** commutator [[(12),(23)],[(23),(34)]] = (14)(23), which kills two levels of radicals ⇒ the quartic formula needs ≥ 3 levels (Ferrari has 3). And [(13)(24),(14)(23)] = () (relint) — V₄ is abelian, so the third commutator level is trivial: the derived series S₄ ⊳ A₄ ⊳ V₄ ⊳ 1 (Żołądek Ex. 7; Kalicki §3.5 counts 24 → 12 → 4 → 1 elements). Akalin's UI labels these levels A (generators, adjacent transpositions), B (first-level commutators, 3-cycles), C (second-level commutators, double transpositions).

**Step 6 (quintic — the stall).** With five roots, [(ijk),(kℓm)] = (jkm) (Ramond eq. 26; concretely [(123),(345)] = (235); Żołądek: [(123),(345)] = (143), [(321),(543)] = (253); Feldbrugge: (143)). The output is again a 3-cycle, so the identity can be fed back into itself: (123) = [(412),(253)] = [[(341),(152)],[(425),(513)]] = … (Ramond eqs 27–28). Group-theoretically: [S₅,S₅] = A₅ and **[A₅,A₅] = A₅** (A₅ is perfect; 3-cycles generate A₅ and each is a commutator of two 3-cycles) — Goldmakher's brute-force version: 60 distinct commutators in S₅, and commutators of those 60 are the same 60, (12345) among them. Hence a formula with N levels of radicals is killed by an N-fold nested commutator loop that still moves s₁→s₂→s₃→s₁, for every N. **Arnold's Theorem:** the monodromy of x(a) for x⁵ + ax + 1 = 0 (or 3w⁵−25w³+60w = z) is the non-solvable S₅, so no function with that branching type is a finite combination of elementary functions and radicals.

**Step 7 (why Galois theory says the same).** Monodromy of an algebraic function ≅ Galois group of its function-field extension (Żołądek Rem. 1; Khovanskii). Radical tower ⇒ monodromy lies in the derived series (Żołądek Thm 2: each ᵏ√ adds a cyclic abelian kernel, each ± × ÷ a subgroup of a product), i.e. "the monodromy of a radical tower of depth N is killed by the N-th derived subgroup" — which is what the nested commutator loops probe experimentally. For the app: a formula of radical depth N is trivialised by loops in G^{(N)} (N-fold commutators); the quintic fails because G^{(N)} = A₅ ≠ 1 for all N.

**Explicit generator data usable in the app** (from Żołądek Lemma 1 / Feldbrugge Fig. 5 / Sottile): fix all coefficients but c₀; the discriminant Δ(c₀) has n−1 simple zeros (in general position); a small loop of c₀ around zero number i, joined to the base point by a non-crossing tether, is a transposition τᵢ of the two roots that collide there. These n−1 transpositions generate Sₙ (Żołądek Lemma 2). A 3-cycle is [τ, τ'] for two of them sharing a root; the nested commutators of Step 6 are then explicit loops in the c₀-plane — nobody has drawn these yet (see §8).

### 2.2 Fred Akalin, "Why is the Quintic Unsolvable?" (2016)

   **Mechanisms worth copying (read from the source):**

- `PathAnimation(p, x1,y1, x2,y2, θ)`: a root moves from (x1,y1) to (x2,y2) along the 3-point polyline through the midpoint displaced perpendicular to the chord by ½·tan θ·|chord| (default θ = π/12), so two roots swapping trace a thin lens and never collide; `invert()` flips θ; `SimultaneousAnimation`, `SequentialAnimation`, and `newCommutatorAnimation(a1,a2)` = a1, a2, a1⁻¹, a2⁻¹.
- `ComplexFormula.root(p)`: keeps the p values of a p-th root in a **stable, continuous order** using a `RotationCounter` that accumulates signed crossings of the negative real axis (sign of the cross product z₁×z₂ decides direction); the branch index is (k + j) mod p. This is exactly the "does √F return?" detector.
- Formulas are composable DAGs (`ComplexFormula` with subformulas), so any candidate expression can be evaluated branch-by-branch along the animation.

## 3. Monodromy and braid pictures

- **Configuration space and the braid group.** Arnold, "On some topological invariants of algebraic functions", _Trudy MMO_ 21 (1970) 27–46 / _Trans. Moscow Math. Soc._ 21 (1970) 30–52: the space Gₙ of monic degree-n polynomials without multiple roots is a K(Bₙ,1); its cohomology is computed there. Żołądek Rem. 2 restates: π₁(ℂⁿ∖Σ) = Bₙ and the monodromy of the universal equation is Bₙ → Sₙ. Esterov & Lang, "Permuting the roots of univariate polynomials whose coefficients depend on parameters", arXiv:2204.14235 (JEMS, 2022/2026): which permutations are realisable by loops when the coefficients depend on parameters with a fixed support — the image of the braid-monodromy map. Salter, "Monodromy of stratified braid groups, II" https://nsalter.science.nd.edu/research/stratifiedbraid2.pdf (2024): equicritical strata of the space of squarefree polynomials (which braidings are possible if the critical-point multiplicities stay fixed), studied through the translation surface of df/f.
- **The braid drawn in ℂ × [0,1].** C. McMullen's Harvard Math 113 (1997) page "Braids" https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/class/harvard/113/97/html/braids.html: p_t(z) = z⁶ + 2t²z − t with t running twice around |t|=1; the roots "trace out a braid in [a,b]×ℂ"; the picture plots time vs Re(root) with over/under crossings, plus an animated GIF. This is the cleanest published instance of the root-braid picture; it is a one-liner to reproduce with any root tracker.
- **Numerical monodromy as a Galois-group computation.** Hauenstein, Rodriguez, Sottile, "Numerical computation of Galois groups", arXiv:1605.07806 (2016; _Found. Comput. Math._ 2018): monodromy permutations from loops encircling each branch point, tracked by homotopy continuation (Bertini/Macaulay2). Worked example: x⁴ − 4x² + t, branch points t ∈ {0,4}, fibre over t=3 is {−√3,−1,1,√3}; the loop around t=0 gives (−1 1), the loop around t=4 gives a product of two transpositions — exactly the kind of "loop around a discriminant point ⇒ named permutation" the app wants to show live. Notes that random loops only ever produce an increasing chain of subgroups (no stopping criterion unless the group is Sₙ).
- **Discriminant pictures.** Cubic z³ + pz + q: Δ = −4p³ − 27q² = 0 is a cuspidal cubic in the real (p,q)-plane (cusp = triple root). Quartic x⁴ + ax² + bx + c: the real discriminant surface is **Arnold's swallowtail**, the tangent developable of t ↦ (−6t², 8t³, −3t⁴) (the curve of polynomials with a triple root), with two cuspidal edges and a self-intersection line (sources: Arnold's _Catastrophe Theory_; the "open swallowtail" literature; e.g. the summary in arXiv:1208.3262 and Shapiro's Arnold-60 article https://staff.math.su.se/shapiro/Articles/Moscow.pdf). In a 1-parameter complex slice (vary one coefficient) the discriminant is a finite set of points — the branch points to loop around (Stein draws them, §2.1); in a 2-parameter real slice it is a curve; nobody has put the complex 1-parameter slice, the loops, and the resulting braid on one screen.
- **Aggregate root-locus pictures** (not monodromy, but rendering ideas): "Polynomiogram" (arXiv:2512.04263, Dec 2025) — two parameters sampled over a domain, mapped to coefficients, all roots plotted, the discriminant appearing as ridges in root density; Dorfsman-Hopkins & Xu, "Searching for Rigidity in Algebraic Starscapes" (J. Math. Arts, 2021–22, arXiv:2107.06328) — algebraic integers plotted and sized by arithmetic invariants, emphasising Galois-theoretic structure.

## 4. Visualising the Galois correspondence

- **Standard diagrams.** Wikipedia "Galois theory" leads with the lattice of ℚ(√2,√3) and its subfields beside the lattice of subgroups of V₄ (SVG on Commons: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lattice_diagram_of_Q_adjoin_the_positive_square_roots_of_2_and_3,_its_subfields,_and_Galois_groups.svg). Worked lattices for ℚ(∛2, ω)/ℚ (S₃) and ℚ(⁴√2, i)/ℚ (D₄) are in K. Conrad, "Some examples of the Galois correspondence" https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/galoistheory/galoiscorrexamples.pdf, "The Galois correspondence at work" https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/galoistheory/galoiscorrthms.pdf, and "Galois groups of cubics and quartics" https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/galoistheory/cubicquartic.pdf (cubic resolvent, discriminant-is-a-square test). LMFDB draws subgroup lattices interactively (§2.7). Carter's _Visual Group Theory_ ch. 10 draws subgroup and field lattices side by side as Hasse diagrams.
- **Roots-with-arrows pictures.** Curtis Bright, "Computing the Galois group of a polynomial" (2013) https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~cbright/reports/computing-galois-group.pdf: "A visual way of expressing Gal(f) is to plot the roots of f on the complex plane and describe permutations of the roots by using arrows to specify which roots are sent where" — Fig. 1 for x³−2 and x³−4x−1 (S₃ vs A₃), Fig. 4 the same for the roots of a resolvent R\_{p,f} with p = x₁+x₂ for x³−2, x⁴+x³+x²+x+1, x⁴+1. Conrad, "Galois groups as permutation groups" https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/galoistheory/galoisaspermgp.pdf (the Sₙ viewpoint), "Recognizing Galois groups Sₙ and Aₙ" https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/galoistheory/galoisSnAn.pdf.
- **Numerical fixed fields (the "watch it become an integer" idea).** This is the classical **Stauduhar resolvent method** (R. Stauduhar, _Math. Comp._ 1973) as implemented in Sage/Magma/PARI: take a polynomial invariant P(x₁..xₙ) of a subgroup H (e.g. x₁x₂ + x₃x₄, or Σ\_{σ∈H} σ(x₁x₂²…)), evaluate it at high-precision numeric roots for each coset of H in the candidate group, form the resolvent ∏(T − P(σ·r)); its coefficients are symmetric ⇒ rational; **Gal(f) ⊂ σHσ⁻¹ iff the resolvent has a rational (integer) root**. Nobody has made this visible. Related recent teaching pieces: Mattman, Robertson-Figaniak, Steele, "Galois theory by calculator", arXiv:2508.18595 (2025) — Galois groups of degree ≤ 5 integer polynomials reduced to "is this integer a square?" and "does this polynomial have an integer root?", with a **Desmos implementation**; "A Worked out Galois Group for the Classroom", _Amer. Math. Monthly_ 2024 https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00029890.2024.2325330 (describes a Galois group through the edges/faces of a tetrahedron; secondary); David Speyer, "Galois theory for PROMYS 2024" https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/speyer/galois-theory-for-promys-2024/ (problem sets; not fetched). Sage: `NumberField(f).galois_group()` returns elements as permutations of the (numerically ordered) roots — https://doc.sagemath.org/html/en/reference/number_fields/sage/rings/number_field/galois_group.html. Hauenstein–Rodriguez–Sottile (§3) compute orbits of the monodromy group on s-tuples of roots via numerical irreducible decomposition — the fibre-product version of the same idea.
- **Explicit fixed-field generators for display.** For H ≤ Gal, the fixed field is generated by the orbit sums Σ\_{σ∈H} σ(m) of monomials m in the roots; the numeric value of such a sum is rational exactly when H is (contained in) the Galois group, and moves under the monodromy of a loop whose permutation is outside H. This gives a live numerical "field lattice": each node shows one such invariant, its numeric value, and whether it is an integer to working precision. (No existing app does this; see §8.)

## 6. Solvable quintics and the "why 3 and 4 work" picture

- D. Dummit, "Solving solvable quintics", _Math. Comp._ 57 (1991) 387–401 https://site.uvm.edu/ddummit/files/2021/04/Solving_Solvable_Quintics__Math_Comp_57_no195_1991__pp_387_401.pdf (+ supplement _Math. Comp._ 59, 1992; notebook `Quintics.nb` on https://site.uvm.edu/ddummit/): an explicit resolvent sextic has a rational root iff the quintic is solvable (Galois group ⊂ F₂₀), and then closed formulas produce the five roots **in cyclic order** — i.e. the radical formula itself exhibits the C₅ ⊂ D₅ ⊂ F₂₀ structure, which an app can show as the roots' arrows.
- Spearman & Williams, "Characterization of solvable quintics x⁵ + ax + b", _Amer. Math. Monthly_ 101 (1994) 986–992 (Ramond's ref. 15). Standard example **x⁵ − 5x + 12**: irreducible, disc = 2¹²·5⁶ a square ⇒ group ⊂ A₅; in fact D₅ (order 10), solvable; radical expression involves the golden ratio and fourth roots (cf. the Math Forums thread https://mathforums.com/t/galois-group-of-x-5-5x-12.28808/). Wikipedia "Galois theory" gives the non-solvable contrast **x⁵ − x − 1** (S₅, via reduction mod 2 and mod 3) and Goldmakher's Exercise 3 (a root of it as an infinitely nested radical).
- Klein's icosahedral solution and Bartlett 2024 (§1.1): the Brioschi one-parameter family whose monodromy is A₅ acting on the 60 sheets of the icosahedral cover; loops around the branch values produce prescribed cyclic permutations. Trott–Adamchik poster (§2.5) illustrates the Hermite/Kronecker/Klein routes with Riemann-surface graphics.
- **The "commutator machinery succeeding" picture** (Katz's video per the Math. Mag. review; Ramond Tables I–II; Akalin's A/B/C button levels; Kalicki §3.4–3.5): cubic — one commutator level kills 1-level formulas, and the second commutator level is trivial (A₃ abelian), so Cardano's 2-level formula survives; quartic — two nested commutator levels kill 2-level formulas, the third level is trivial (V₄ abelian), so Ferrari's 3-level formula survives; quintic — every level is A₅ again. Akalin's "find the first operation that rules out this formula" is the interactive form of this: at degree 4 the search terminates with "no operation rules out the 3-level formula"; at degree 5 the C-level 3-cycles rule out every finite nesting.

## 7. Rendering and animation techniques already in use

- **Root tracking along a coefficient path.** Stein: Aberth–Ehrlich re-solve on every drag event (JSXGraph drag) — with roots matched to the previous frame implicitly by the solver's warm start; Akalin: roots are the _inputs_ (dragged), coefficients derived, so no tracking is needed; radical branches are tracked by the rotation counter (§2.2). The rigorous alternative is predictor–corrector **homotopy continuation** along the user's path (Hauenstein–Rodriguez–Sottile use Bertini; Timme's `HomotopyContinuation.jl`; Verschelde's robust path tracker https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04984), with step-size control keyed to the distance to the discriminant. For a WebGL/TS app the practical scheme is: Aberth on the polynomial at parameter t₀, then Newton-correct each root at t₀+Δt with Δt chosen so that the predicted step is ≪ the minimal root separation, and detect/refuse discriminant crossings.
- **Path shapes for swapping points.** Akalin: 3-point polyline (chord midpoint offset by ½·tan(π/12)·|chord|, i.e. a shallow lens; clockwise/counter-clockwise variants); Ramond Fig. 1: two arcs of a lens for a transposition, three arcs for a 3-cycle, and the untouched root doing a small loop; Kalicki: straight lines (collision-prone); relint: straight interpolation with sigmoid easing σ(t) between root points. The classical braid picture (McMullen) uses the _actual_ root trajectories, not an abstract arc.
- **Domain colouring / phase portraits for the backdrop.** Wegert-style phase portraits (roots = points where all hues meet) with WebGL fragment shaders: R. Reusser's Observable notebooks "Domain Coloring with Adaptive Contouring" https://observablehq.com/@rreusser/adaptive-domain-coloring and "Adaptive Contouring in Fragment Shaders" https://observablehq.com/@rreusser/locally-scaled-domain-coloring-part-1-contour-plots (regl, screen-space derivatives to keep contour density constant under zoom); J. C. Ponce Campuzano, _Complex Analysis — A Visual and Interactive Introduction_ https://complex-analysis.com/content/domain_coloring.html (p5.js/GLSL applets); https://www.dynamicmath.xyz/domain-coloring/ ; GeoGebra HSV phase portraits https://www.geogebra.org/m/neJs5awW ; Kranich, "GPU-based visualization of domain-coloured algebraic Riemann surfaces", arXiv:1507.04571 (2015; geometry-shader / WebGL multi-target rendering of the Riemann surface of P(w,z)=0 — the 3D version of the sheets picture). The suite's own `@cas/gpu` phase-portrait shaders and `@cas/expr` evaluator cover this already.
- **Animating a permutation of n points smoothly.** D3 transitions (https://d3js.org/d3-transition, Bostock "Working with Transitions" https://bost.ocks.org/mike/transition/) interpolate positions but do not avoid collisions; for abstract permutations the accepted trick is the arc/lens swap above; for monodromy permutations the honest animation is along the real root paths of the loop (which is what makes the picture a proof rather than an illustration). A fading canvas layer (draw with low alpha each frame) or an explicit polyline per root gives trails; McMullen's [0,1]×ℂ plot gives the braid.

## 8. What the best existing treatment does, and what none of them do

**Best written**: Ramond 2020 — explicit permutation identities at each level, the F/G/H hierarchy, Table I/II, and the self-feeding identity [(ijk),(kℓm)] = (jkm) that makes the quintic stall obvious; supplementary animations of its figures. **Best rigorous**: Żołądek 2000 (+ Vrij 2022 for the Δθ computation). **Best video**: Katz (c. 2020); the "not all wrong" video (2021) is the popular retelling. **Best interactive**: Akalin 2016 — candidate formulas evaluated live along animated root swaps, branch tracking with a rotation counter, operation buttons organised by commutator depth, an algorithm that finds the killing operation. **Best "look at the branch points"**: Stein 2019 (discriminant zeros appear while you drag a coefficient). **Best group pictures**: LMFDB subgroup lattices and Group Explorer Cayley diagrams. **Best braid picture**: McMullen's 1997 GIF.

What **none** of them do (each is a concrete opportunity for the app):

1. **Loops drawn in coefficient space, roots tracked by continuation.** Every demo moves the _roots_ and derives the coefficient loops by Vieta (the only direction that is trivially continuous). Nobody lets the user draw or select a loop in the cⱼ-plane (around chosen discriminant points) and computes the induced permutation; Stein comes closest (drag a coefficient, see the discriminant zeros) but has no loop object, no recording, no permutation readout.
2. **The discriminant locus on screen.** No demo draws Δ = 0 as a slice (points in the c₀-plane; the cusp curve for the cubic's (p,q); the swallowtail for the quartic) with the base point and the loops tethered to it.
3. **Nested commutators as a structured object.** Akalin flattens them into buttons A/B/C; Ramond writes (123) = [[(341),(152)],[(425),(513)]]; nobody draws the commutator as a tree of loops (γ₁γ₂γ₁⁻¹γ₂⁻¹ with each γ itself expandable), or shows, side by side, the depth-N loop in coefficient space, the depth-N radical tower it defeats, and the 3-cycle it produces.
4. **The radical tower as a live object.** Akalin evaluates fixed candidate formulas; nobody lets the user _type_ a formula (tree with radical nodes), colours each radical node by whether it closed along the current loop, and reports the first depth at which it fails.
5. **The A₅ = [A₅,A₅] step made visible.** Goldmakher's "60 of 14400, then the same 60 again" and Vrij's Python check are printed numbers; no one shows the derived series as shrinking highlighted subsets of a Cayley diagram / cycle graph of S₅ (Group Explorer could, by hand), or the stall at A₅ as "the same set lights up again".
6. **The Galois correspondence numerically.** No app evaluates H-invariant symmetric functions of the numeric roots and shows them becoming integers (the resolvent test), nor links a subgroup-lattice node to an intermediate field with a concrete generator, nor shows how a loop's permutation moves the invariant when it is outside H.
7. **The braid in ℂ×[0,1] for the commutator loop.** McMullen's picture exists only for a single parameter circle; no one renders the braid of a commutator loop (its 4 segments), where the "cancellation" of windings for radicals and the _non_-cancellation for roots can be seen as braid words.
8. **Solvable-vs-general quintic side by side.** Dummit's cyclic-order roots, x⁵−5x+12 (D₅) versus x⁵−x−1 (S₅), and the Brioschi/icosahedron family are treated in separate places; no interactive shows a solvable quintic's monodromy stalling inside F₂₀ while the general one reaches S₅.
9. **GPU backdrop.** All demos are dots on a white plane; none draws the phase portrait of p, so the roots are not seen as the points where colours meet, and the discriminant is not seen as the moment two colour singularities merge.
10. **Honest labelling of the numerics.** None distinguishes "this permutation was decided" (winding number, root separation bound) from "the tracker guessed"; a continuation that steps across the discriminant silently produces a wrong permutation — Sottile's paper is explicit that uncertified continuation is the weak point.
